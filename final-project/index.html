01 — Research Dashboard

My Approach

Driving Question

Can AI be used for good, and could it be something we work together with, instead of something we hate before we understand it?

What I Studied

The Pinterest ad "How did they do it." I studied it for the emotional tone and how it made me want to go offline and appreciate the small, silly parts of being human. Apple's "Think Different," for the way it shows the humanness in all of us and our drive to create. And a ton of Kling 3.0 tutorials, which is where I figured out how to make the motion.

What It Produced

A still-to-motion workflow I built myself. The main thing I figured out is that locking the visual in a still first gave me full control before I spent any motion credits. I also learned that AI defaults to looking too polished, and that naming a real, specific thing works way better than any adjective.

Where I Pushed Back

I tested the Kling tutorial techniques on my own footage instead of just taking their word for it. I kept what worked and dropped the rest. I also didn't trust the AI's "realistic" default. The first clips looked too clean, so I treated that as something to fix. And I only committed to the single starting frame after watching the start-and-end-frame version fall apart.

Where I Landed

The research gave me a pretty clear answer to my question. AI can actually be a real collaborator when you give it specific, concrete limits. It's genuinely useful for raw material, speed, and options. Where it can't go is taste, intent, and judgment. Those stayed mine the whole time. So it's more like working with it than competing with it.

02 — Process

Workflow + Prompts

01 — Workflow

How It Went

First, Claude writes and refines a prompt with my feedback. Then ChatGPT Image 2.0 turns it into a starting frame, which is a still I can control exactly. Claude writes a second prompt made for Kling, built off that frame, and Kling 3.0 animates it into a clip. After that I put everything together in Premiere Pro, with an ElevenLabs voiceover reading my script and one ambient sound bed underneath. Locking the visual in a still first meant I knew exactly what I had before I spent my limited Kling credits on the motion.

02 — Closing Block · Added to Every Motion Prompt
Shot on an iPhone 8 — slightly imperfect focus, not color corrected, casual framing. Real time motion, not slow motion. Camera completely locked, no movement, no zoom, no drift. Natural micro-tremor as if handheld. Candid, not posed. Photorealistic, 16:9.

Naming a real phone worked way better than telling it to look "realistic." The iPhone 8 adds in some imperfect lighting and exposure on its own, so the clips actually looked real instead of staged.

03 — Iterations & Discoveries

Iterations & Discoveries

Making a still starting frame before animating anything let me lock in the visual direction, like the composition, lighting, and mood, while it was still cheap to change. AI video defaults to looking too good, and getting something human and nostalgic out of it meant adding the imperfection back in on purpose.

04 — What Did Not Work

What Did Not Work

At first I made both a start frame and an end frame and asked Kling to animate between them. I thought that would give me more control, but it didn't. Kling kept losing the thread trying to get from one to the other, and the motion just fell apart. So I dropped the end frame and let the model build the motion out from one strong starting image.

05 — What I'd Do Differently

What I'd Do Differently

I'd learn the lesson about restraints sooner. If you control the AI too much, the output sometimes gets worse, not better. By the end I was just controlling the intent, like the iPhone 8 block, a locked camera, and one strong starting frame, and then letting the model do the rest. I'd start there next time instead of getting there after a bunch of rounds of fighting it.

03 — The Outcome

The Outcome

What I Made

The Video

A 40-second AI-made video about human creativity. It has a nostalgic feel and a voiceover from a script I co-wrote with Claude, and at the end it reveals itself as an ad made with Claude. Every shot started as a still frame from ChatGPT Image 2.0, then got animated in Kling 3.0, with ElevenLabs voicing my script. It all sits over one Pixabay ambient bed of kids laughing and waves at the beach, and I edited it in Premiere Pro.

How AI Shaped It

AI's Role

AI helped with almost everything. Claude co-wrote the script and helped me write and refine the prompts. ChatGPT Image 2.0 made every starting frame, Kling 3.0 animated them, and ElevenLabs voiced the final read. What it never did was decide what the piece meant, how it should feel, or when something was actually good. That was all me.

The Script · Co-Written with Claude

Before we had words for it, we were already doing it. Nobody teaches a child to make a mark. Nobody explains to a teenager why a feeling needs a song. Nobody tells us to build things we don't need, or make things nobody asked for. We just do. It passes through us. From one pair of hands to the next. From one generation to the next. Now we create together.

My Authorship

My Authorship

I directed and edited the whole thing. I gave Claude the idea and then shaped every line it gave back. The starting frame I controlled exactly. I made deliberate choices throughout to push the output away from its clean default and toward something more imperfect and human. I picked the music, timed every cut to the voiceover, and wrote the end cards myself.

04 — Reflection

What I Learned

The Click Moment

The click moment was realizing the early clips looked wrong because they were too perfect. Naming a real, slightly older camera like the iPhone 8 did what "make it realistic" couldn't. You have to give it something real to copy instead of an adjective.

What I Learned

AI is useful and good, but it can't do everything for you. My professor calls it "glasses for your brain," and that's exactly how it felt. It helps you see better, it doesn't see for you. One the thing I didn't expect to get better at was patience. I had to learn to sit with not getting it right away and just keep trying instead of forcing it.

Then

I went in chasing a feeling. It was that specific ache from the Pinterest ad that made me want to go offline and notice the small, silly parts of being alive. I figured the AI tools would mostly just execute a vision I already had finished in my head.

Now

I came out with something that makes its point by being the thing it's about. It shows the idea instead of just saying it, then admits at the end that it was made with AI and asks you to judge it by how it made you feel. My view of AI didn't really change. I started in the middle and I'm still there. I just have specific, first-hand proof for it now instead of a vague feeling.

What I'd Keep Exploring

If I kept going, I'd want to keep testing exactly where the line is between directing what a tool should do and getting in the way of what it's actually good at. That tension is what this whole project ended up living in.
