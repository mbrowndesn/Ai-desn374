The Film

Born to Create is a 60-second film generated almost entirely with AI video tools — and it argues, gently, that the urge to make things is older than language itself.

It opens on a hand pressing into wet sand, cuts through cave paintings and small human acts of making, and builds to an old man turning a carved wooden bird in his hands. Then the screen goes white and a single line appears: "Now we create together." The film ends by telling you the truth — that it was made with AI — instead of hiding it.

That disclosure isn't a disclaimer tacked on the end. It's the whole point. The film only works if it's honest about its own making.

02

The Question

Case Study · Inquiry

Can an ad made with AI honestly celebrate human creativity — and will people trust it if it tells the truth about how it was made?

Where the project landed

AI is the newest tool in a line that runs back to charcoal on a cave wall. The film doesn't pretend a machine made art on its own — it shows a human hand steering the tool, in the open. Honesty became the creative strategy, not a compromise.

Research Dashboard

Approach & Engagement

The film makes an argument, so the research had to test whether that argument holds up. Three threads ran in parallel: how audiences actually feel about AI ads, why emotional storytelling works, and how the AI-video tools themselves evolved to the point where one student could make a film like this at all.

What the evidence kept saying

1

People can tell. Viewers spontaneously identify most AI ads as AI — the only one that fooled them was made by a professional through heavy iterative editing. Craft is the difference.

2

People want honesty. A majority of consumers want advertisers to disclose AI, and transparency can turn discomfort into curiosity rather than backlash.

3

Emotion has an architecture. Effective ads hook with emotion early, then sustain with meaningful, empathetic moments — almost exactly the shape of this script.

How I verified & questioned
  • Separated showcase from sustainable. Sora's 2026 shutdown was a reminder that a flashy demo isn't the same as a working tool — I kept claims tied to what actually ships.
  • Weighted peer-review over blogs. For any factual claim about the field, I leaned on the Springer studies, not timeline listicles, and treated blog timelines as leads to check, not proof.
  • Pressure-tested my own thesis. The NielsenIQ finding could have killed the project — if people always spot AI, why try? It reframed the goal toward craft instead.
How research shaped the film
  • Disclosure end card ← IAB + CMR + NielsenIQ (audiences want and reward honesty)
  • Heavy, hand-edited iteration ← NielsenIQ (only crafted AI work passed)
  • Emotional arc & pacing ← AMA neuroscience (hook early, sustain with meaning)
  • Cave-art opening ← archaeology (the ancient creative urge, factually grounded)
  • Short shots + consistency features ← scene-stability research (manage drift)

Process

Workflow · Iteration · Pivots
01

Script & storyboard

Locked the 11-shot arc and the white-screen ending before generating anything.

02

Frame generation

Built start & end frames in ChatGPT / Firefly to control each shot's composition.

03

Image-to-video

Animated frames in Kling, using start/end frames to steer motion and hold the look.

04

Sound & edit

Voiceover, a sparse Suno underscore, and ambient layers cut against the picture.

What worked

  • Start/end frame control. Generating both ends of a shot in Firefly first, then letting Kling fill the motion, gave far more consistent results than text-to-video alone.
  • Keeping shots short. Coherence held better in brief clips, so I edited around the tools' limits instead of fighting them.
  • One real shot. Filming the opening beach moment on a phone anchored the whole AI film in something undeniably human — the most "AI-design" decision in the project.

What didn't — and the pivots

  • The "too AI" sheen. Early shots had a synthetic gloss. Pivot: commit to one warm, naturalistic style across all 11 shots instead of treating each as an experiment.
  • Scene drift. Kling wandered between frames — faces and objects shifted. Pivot: shorter shots, consistency features, and tighter start/end frames.
  • Suno wanted to write a song. It kept adding melody and structure. Pivot: custom-mode instrumental prompts for an underscore that disappears under the voice.
A prompt that earned its place
// Suno — instrumental underscore, not a song
Style: sparse fingerstyle acoustic guitar, ambient, intimate,
  no drums, no vocals, no melody, slow, warm,
  underscore for a documentary, room-tone feel, restrained
// Generated 5–8 takes; kept the one that "forgot it was a song."

Authorship & Synthesis

Where my judgment shows up

The honest line between what the tools generated and what I decided. AI produced a lot of raw material; almost none of it survived untouched.

The AI did

Raw material

  • Generated frames and motion for each shot from my prompts
  • Produced multiple takes of every shot and music bed
  • Offered voiceover options and ambient sound candidates
  • Surfaced research leads I then verified myself
I did

Every decision

  • Wrote and locked the script and emotional arc
  • Rejected the synthetic look and chose one naturalistic style
  • Filmed the opening shot for real against the AI grain
  • Cut, sequenced, and mixed the sound so music sits under the voice
  • Made the call that disclosure is the climax, not a footnote
06

Reflection

Learning & Growth

The click moment: I stopped trying to make AI look human — and started letting a human hand show, out in the open.

Then

I assumed a good AI film was one where you couldn't tell. The win was hiding the seams — making the machine convincing enough to pass as something else.

Now

The win is the opposite: human judgment steering the tool, in the open. Disclosure isn't a weakness to manage — it's what makes the work trustworthy and the work itself more honest.
